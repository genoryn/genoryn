Quantum security

Quantum-resistant cryptocurrency, explained.

A quantum-resistant cryptocurrency (also called post-quantum or quantum-safe) is one whose coins cannot be stolen, and whose private data cannot be decrypted, by a quantum computer. This page explains which cryptography a quantum computer breaks, which it does not, what that means for Bitcoin and for privacy coins, and how Genoryn Krypton (GNR) is built against it.

The short answer

  • Elliptic curves break. Shor's algorithm, run on a large enough quantum computer, computes a private key from a public key. That covers the signatures that guard coins on Bitcoin, Ethereum, Monero and Zcash.
  • Hash functions hold. Grover's algorithm gives only a quadratic speed-up against hashes, so a 256-bit hash keeps a 128-bit security margin.
  • Privacy is the urgent part. Encrypted data recorded today can be decrypted later ("harvest now, decrypt later"), and no upgrade can undo that.
  • Genoryn Krypton uses no elliptic curve to protect coins or privacy: spending is a zk-STARK proof over hash functions, and payment data is encrypted with ML-KEM, NIST's post-quantum standard.

What a quantum computer breaks, and what it does not

Two quantum algorithms matter. Shor's algorithm solves the mathematical problems behind RSA and elliptic curves outright. Grover's algorithm speeds up brute-force search, which only halves the effective strength of a hash function or a symmetric key.

When does it matter?

No quantum computer today can break a 256-bit elliptic curve, and nobody knows when one will. Two facts make waiting risky all the same:

  • Standards bodies are already moving. NIST published its first post-quantum standards (FIPS 203, 204 and 205) in August 2024, and its transition plan proposes to stop using quantum-vulnerable public-key algorithms by 2035.
  • A blockchain never forgets. Everything written to a public chain stays there. Whoever records it today can attack it whenever the hardware exists.

Money meant to hold value for decades has to be safe for decades, not only today.

Two different risks: theft and loss of privacy

Theft. If coins are guarded by an elliptic-curve signature, anyone who can run Shor's algorithm on the public key can sign for them. A chain can change its signature scheme later, but only for coins whose owners move them in time. Coins that are lost, forgotten or never moved stay exposed.

Loss of privacy. Privacy coins encrypt what they hide. If that encryption depends on elliptic curves, transactions recorded today can be decrypted in the future, exposing amounts and recipients after the fact. This cannot be repaired by an upgrade, because the old data is already public. A private currency has to be post-quantum from its first block.

Is Bitcoin quantum-safe?

Not today. Bitcoin's coins are protected by ECDSA and Schnorr signatures on the secp256k1 curve. How exposed a coin is depends on whether its public key is visible:

  • Already visible: early pay-to-public-key outputs, addresses that have been spent from and reused, and Taproot outputs. Several million bitcoin sit in such outputs.
  • Visible when spent: every other coin reveals its public key in the transaction that spends it, while that transaction waits to be confirmed.

Bitcoin's mining is not the weak point: it relies on SHA-256, which a quantum computer does not break. Bitcoin's developers are discussing post-quantum signature upgrades; moving to them would require holders to move their coins.

Genoryn Krypton keeps Bitcoin's monetary design, 21,000,000 coins and halvings, and connects to bitcoin directly: the wallet exchanges BTC and GNR with atomic swaps, so value can move into a quantum-resistant system with no exchange and no custody.

What "quantum-resistant" does not mean

  • It means resistant to the known quantum algorithms. Nobody can prove that no better attack will ever be found, which is why the encryption is hybrid and the algorithms are replaceable.
  • It is not a statement about code quality. Genoryn Krypton has had an internal audit and a privately reported external review, whose findings are fixed, but no full independent audit yet.
  • It is not investment advice. Mining or holding GNR is not a promise of value.
